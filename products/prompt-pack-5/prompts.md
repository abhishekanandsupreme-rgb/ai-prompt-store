## Story Structure & Plotting (Prompts 1–8)

### 1. The 3-Act Outline
```
You are a developmental editor with two decades of fiction craft. My story idea: [PREMISE]. Structure it as a 3-act outline with the inciting incident at 10–12%, the midpoint reversal at 50%, and the climax at 90% of the story. Give each act a one-paragraph summary PLUS a scene list of 10–15 beats total across the acts — each beat one line: what happens, and the protagonist's emotional state as it changes (name the shift, e.g., hope → suspicion). Mark where the protagonist makes an irreversible choice. Flag any stretch of 2+ beats where they only react — passive stretches kill manuscripts.
```

### 2. Stakes Ladder Audit
```
Act as a story structure expert. Analyze my plot: [SUMMARIZE PLOT]. Identify the stakes ladder: list every stakes escalation point in order with its percentage position — are stakes rising every 25% of the story, or is there a plateau? Point out any stretch where the protagonist is passive for more than one beat. Then rewrite 3 beats to force active choices with irreversible consequences: each rewrite shows the original beat, the new beat where the protagonist DECIDES rather than reacts, and what that choice forecloses. End with the one change that most raises the story's ceiling.
```

### 3. Midpoint Failure Diagnosis
```
You are a plotting coach who has rescued 200 stalled novels. I'm stuck at the midpoint of [STORY — describe where you are]. Diagnose: the 4 most common midpoint failures that fit my symptoms (stakes unclear, protagonist solving the wrong problem, revelation too early, no cost to progress), identify which one I have based on what I've told you — and say which I probably DON'T have. Give 3 divergent ways forward: each with the ripple effect it creates for my ending (name at least one thing each path makes impossible), and the kind of ending it points toward. Include the 10-minute exercise that breaks the deadlock today.
```

### 4. Genre Promises Checklist
```
Act as a genre specialist who knows what readers of [GENRE] are owed. List the 5 obligatory scenes and 5 conventions of [GENRE]. Check my synopsis against them: [PASTE SYNOPSIS] — mark each as: delivered ✓, delivered weakly ~, or missing ✗, with the line from my synopsis (or the gap). Show how to satisfy the unmet promises WITHOUT becoming formulaic: 2 options each that subvert or invert the expectation rather than skipping it. End with the warning: which missing promise would make [GENRE] readers abandon the book, and which one is safe to skip if subverted well.
```

### 5. Subplot Architecture
```
You are a subplots architect. My main plot is [MAIN PLOT]. Design 3 subplots that mirror or complicate the theme [THEME]: one relationship subplot, one internal subplot, one world subplot. For each: how it starts (the hook in chapter terms), where it crosses the main plot — twice, at specific percentage marks (say when), how it amplifies the main plot's tension at each crossing, and how it resolves in a way that changes the ending's meaning. Each subplot must be capable of generating 3–5 scenes on its own. Flag any subplot that's actually just backstory in disguise.
```

### 6. Ending Stress Test
```
Act as an endings specialist. My story is [PREMISE] and my planned ending is [ENDING]. Test it against 4 criteria: (1) EARNED — what earlier moments set this up? name them, and if you can't, list what to plant now; (2) SURPRISING BUT INEVITABLE — could a reader predict it by chapter 3? does it feel like the only possible ending in retrospect?; (3) PROPORTIONAL — does the climax's scale match the stakes built?; (4) CAUSED — does the protagonist's own choice bring it about, or does coincidence rescue/doom them? If it fails any test, propose 2 stronger endings — each with what to plant earlier to earn it, and what each ending says about the theme.
```

### 7. Series Arc Design
```
You are a series planner who knows book-2 sag kills series. My standalone story: [SUMMARIZE]. Design a 3-book arc: the overarching conflict (big enough for 3 books — say why it can't resolve in one), what each book resolves vs. carries forward, the per-book stakes that escalate (each book's stakes must be a different kind of loss, not just "more"), and the character engine — the internal wound that heals (or deepens) across the series. Flag the biggest book-2 risk: what typically sags (the "empire building" stretch) and the 2 techniques that prevent it. End with the promise the series makes to readers and how the finale pays it.
```

### 8. Tension Curve Surgery
```
Act as a pacing surgeon. Here is my chapter-by-chapter outline: [PASTE OUTLINE]. Chart the tension curve: rate each chapter's tension 1–10 and mark the 3 flattest stretches (2+ consecutive chapters without a rise or turn). For each flat stretch, prescribe ONE: a scene cut (say which), a merge, a timeline compression, or a new complication — and show the revised beat sequence for that stretch. Then check the macro-rhythm: where the two biggest valleys sit relative to the 25%/60% marks, and whether readers get a breather before the climax. Output the revised full outline with changes marked.
```

## Character Development (Prompts 9–16)

### 9. Protagonist Profile, Deep
```
You are a character psychologist who builds people, not resumes. Build a full profile for my protagonist: [BASIC DESCRIPTION]. Include: the childhood wound that formed them (an incident, not a category), want vs. need (stated in one line each — and they must conflict), the lie they believe (the false thing that keeps them stuck), how the wound shaped their career and relationships in visible ways, their 3 defense mechanisms (each with the behavior a reader would SEE), and the exact moment in the story when the lie cracks — what triggers it and what they do first. Ground everything in 3 behaviors we'll actually see on the page, not internal monologue claims.
```

### 10. Dialogue Voice Fingerprints
```
Act as a character voice coach. Create distinct dialogue voices for these 3 characters: [DESCRIBE CHARACTERS]. For each: vocabulary level and register, sentence length rhythm (short bursts vs. flowing clauses), pet phrases (2 — usable as recognition markers), what they NEVER say (and what they say instead), the topic that changes their speech pattern (and how it changes). Then write a 5-line dialogue sample where all three discuss [SHARED TOPIC] — strip the attributions from a copy of it: the reader should identify each speaker from voice alone. Tell me which two are still too close, and the one adjustment that splits them.
```

### 11. Antagonist as Dark Mirror
```
You are an antagonist designer. My hero is [HERO — their belief and wound in a line]. Create an antagonist who is the hero's dark mirror: (1) the backstory that justifies their goal — a wound parallel to the hero's, (2) why they believe they're right — their philosophy stated so convincingly a reader nods, (3) the way their philosophy opposes the hero's — the same question, opposite answers, (4) 3 escalating moves (each move should be something the hero could have made in a worse mood), and (5) the humanizing detail that makes readers uncomfortable agreeing with them. The confrontation must be an argument the story settles, not just a fight.
```

### 12. Supporting Cast with Functions
```
Act as a supporting cast strategist. My protagonist's journey: [SUMMARIZE]. Cast 6 supporting characters, each assigned a narrative function: ally, mirror (shows who the protagonist could become), threshold guardian, complicator, confidant, foil. For each: how they serve the theme, their own mini-arc (what changes for them, in one line — they're people, not props), the scene where they earn their place in the story (cut them if they can't fill it), and the relationship to the protagonist at start vs. end. Then the test: which two could merge into one character without loss — say which, and the merged version.
```

### 13. Arc Tracker
```
You are a character arc specialist. Track my protagonist's arc through [STORY]: [PASTE CHAPTER SUMMARY]. Map: starting belief → pressure events (list them, in order) → the dark night (the moment the old belief costs everything) → the new truth they act on. Then audit for out-of-character moments: identify any scene where they act to serve the plot instead of their psychology — list each, with why it's out of character, and a rewrite where the plot bends to the character instead (change the circumstance, not the person). End with the arc's weakest link: the step that needs one more scene to convince.
```

### 14. Central Relationship Design
```
Act as a relationship dynamics expert. Design the central relationship of my story between [CHARACTER A] and [CHARACTER B]: (1) the initial misimpression each has of the other, (2) the friction engine — the underlying conflict that generates scenes (needs to be structural: opposing goals or values, not just misunderstanding), (3) 3 escalation moments, (4) the false defeat — the moment the relationship seems beyond repair, and why it isn't, and (5) the changed dynamic at the end. Then write the key confrontation scene: 300 words of dialogue with subtext — what each says, what each means, and the one line where the truth slips out.
```

### 15. Grief on the Page
```
You are a grief and emotion consultant who writes loss without cliché. My character experiences [MAJOR LOSS/EVENT]. Write the emotional aftermath realistically: (1) the 5 stages they move through — non-linearly, with regressions (say where the story doubles back), (2) what they suppress and how the suppression shows (behavior, not statement), (3) the trigger that breaks through — a mundane object or moment, not a grand one, (4) how grief warps their decisions in the plot — name 2 plot points that are actually grief talking, and (5) how other characters misread them at each stage. No "time heals" arcs — the grief changes shape, it doesn't vanish.
```

### 16. Character Sheet Interrogation
```
Act as a character sheet auditor with a casting director's eye. Here is my character sheet: [PASTE SHEET]. Interrogate it: (1) what's cliché — list every entry you've seen in 100 other sheets, (2) what's vague — entries that could describe anyone ("loyal to friends"), (3) the unexplored contradiction — the two entries that fight each other, which is the good news (contradictions are gold) and how to mine it, (4) what an actor would grab onto — the playable, specific details. Rewrite the 3 weakest entries into specific, playable traits: each rewrite in 2 sentences with something a reader can picture.
```

## Dialogue & Prose Craft (Prompts 17–25)

### 17. Subtext Rewrite
```
You are a dialogue editor. Rewrite this conversation to carry subtext: [PASTE DIALOGUE]. Keep the surface topic identical, but make each character want something they won't say directly. Techniques to apply: interruption (who cuts whom, and why), deflection (answering a different question), objects in the scene (business that reveals nerves), and the line that says the opposite of what's meant. Show the rewrite with stage business, then annotate the 4 key subtext moves — quote the line, name the technique, state what's underneath. End with the rule the original broke most (usually: characters saying exactly what they feel).
```

### 18. Voice Split for Same-Sounding Characters
```
Act as a dialogue mechanic. My characters sound alike: [PASTE 3 EXCERPTS]. Diagnose the sameness: is it syntax (everyone speaks in complete sentences), content (everyone makes the same observations), or rhythm (everyone has the same beat pattern)? Name it precisely. Then rewrite each excerpt so each character has an unmistakable fingerprint, and produce a reusable voice card for each: 5-line sample, 3 signature moves, 1 never-do. Then the shibboleth test: write a 3-line exchange between two of them on a neutral topic — if a beta reader could still swap the speakers, tell me which distinction is failing and why.
```

### 19. Three Prose Voices
```
You are a prose stylist fluent in the great registers. Analyze this passage's current rhythm and sound: [PASTE PARAGRAPH]. Then rewrite it 3 ways: (1) sparse — Hemingway: short declaratives, nouns and verbs doing the work, adjectives on a diet; (2) lyrical — Le Guin: flowing rhythm, concrete sensory detail, sentence-length music; (3) propulsive — thriller pacing: fragments, action verbs, cliff-hanging sentence ends. For each version: 2 sentences on the technique behind it — the specific moves (sentence length pattern, word class choices) so I can choose deliberately and repeat it. End with which voice best serves my [GENRE] and why.
```

### 20. Show, Don't Tell Conversion
```
Act as a showing-vs-telling coach. Here is my draft: [PASTE 300 WORDS]. Convert every telling sentence into a showing moment: cut filter words (felt, seemed, realized, noticed), externalize internal states through action, body language, dialogue choice, or what the POV character notices in the scene (attention IS emotion), and make description do double duty (environment that reveals the noticer). Present: (1) the clean rewrite, (2) a conversion log — each told → shown pair listed, and (3) the one place where telling is CORRECT (summary transitions between scenes) — showing everything bloats books; know the exceptions.
```

### 21. Summary to Scene
```
You are a scene-level dramatist. This summary needs to be a scene: [PASTE SUMMARY]. Write it as a full scene with: goal-conflict-disaster structure (the POV character enters wanting X, meets resistance, leaves with a worse problem), sensory grounding in [SETTING] — 3 details per sense, weighted to the dominant one, dialogue that escalates (each exchange raises the stakes or shifts the power), and a turn at the end. Include what the POV character notices that reveals their emotional filter (fear notices exits; guilt notices eyes). Length: [TARGET] words. Mark where I should slow time (the beats-per-second stretch) and where to compress.
```

### 22. Fight Choreography
```
Act as a fight/action choreographer. Write a fight between [CHARACTER A, with skill level] and [CHARACTER B, skill level] in [LOCATION]. Rules: (1) ground it in the location's hazards — the fight uses the environment, (2) show skill differences through tactics and target choice, never adjectives ("masterful" is not choreography), (3) break action with dialogue and interior thought at realistic intervals — 2–3 beats max between breaths, (4) make the winner pay a cost — injury, loss, or reveal, and (5) the fight must change the relationship or the plot, or cut it. 600 words. Then note: the moment the fight was actually decided (usually 3 moves before it ends).
```

### 23. Emotional Filter Description
```
You are a description specialist. My setting is [SETTING]. Write 3 description passages of the same place through 3 different emotional filters — afraid, nostalgic, furious — each under 100 words. Rules: same location, same time of day; only the DETAILS change, filtered by what a person in that state would notice (fear notices sounds and exits; nostalgia notices what's gone; fury notices what's wrong). No naming the emotion anywhere in the passages. After the 3, extract the reusable principle: the 3-line checklist for writing "emotion through environment" that I can apply to any setting.
```

### 24. Chapter-Turn Engineering
```
Act as a line editor for impact. Here is my scene ending: [PASTE]. Optimize the final 3 lines for a chapter-turn: (1) cut anything that softens the landing — trailing thoughts, tidy wrap-ups, emotional summaries, (2) reposition the strongest word to the end of the line (endings echo), and (3) offer 3 alternative closing lines with different hooks: a question (new mystery), a threat (implied danger), a revelation (reframe of what came before). For each alternative: the reason a reader turns the page instead of setting the bookmark. Recommend one for [GENRE] and explain the genre logic.
```

### 25. Filter-Word Extermination
```
You are a filter-word exterminator. Edit this passage: [PASTE 500 WORDS]. Remove: filter words (saw, felt, seemed, realized, watched, noticed), crutch words (suddenly, very, just, really), and redundant stage direction (she reached out her hand and took the cup). Keep meaning and voice intact — target a 15% word-count reduction, not a butchery. Present: (1) the clean version, and (2) the 5 worst habits found, each with my original line as the example and the fix pattern — so I catch them at draft time. Note the one filter word you kept, and why it earned its place.
```

## Worldbuilding & Setting (Prompts 26–30)

### 26. Power System Rules
```
You are a worldbuilding architect in the Sanderson tradition: magic with costs and limits. My world has [MAGIC/TECH/POWER SYSTEM — describe]. Design its rules: (1) the source — where it comes from and why that matters socially, (2) the costs — what using it takes (the cost must be able to drive plot), (3) the limits — what it can never do, stated so a reader can predict outcomes, (4) who can use it and why — the access gate, (5) the exploitable loophole — the rule a clever character could bend, and (6) the societal consequences: economy, law, religion, warfare — 2 concrete examples each. Then: 3 plot problems this system creates and 3 it solves. If the system can solve any problem, it's broken — say so.
```

### 27. Sensory Research Kit
```
Act as a setting researcher. My story is set in [PLACE, ERA]. Build a sensory research kit: (1) 10 period-accurate details across smell, sound, texture, and taste — the specifics that were everyday then and are unimaginable now, (2) 5 social rules a character of [CLASS/GENDER] would never break — and the consequence of breaking each, (3) 2 anachronisms writers of this era typically make — the ones that pull readers out, (4) 3 anchor objects — things that could sit in any scene and do work, and (5) 1 detail that's true but sounds wrong — the one readers will think is a mistake. Mark which details need verification for accuracy.
```

### 28. Culture Design
```
You are a culture designer for fiction. Create the culture of [GROUP] in my world: (1) the values hierarchy — what they'd die for, what they'd trade away, (2) taboos (3) — with the origin logic for each, (3) the honor system — how status is earned and lost, (4) greetings and partings — what the ritual reveals about the value system, (5) curse words — what they invoke says what the culture fears, (6) funeral rites — the single most revealing ritual in any culture, and (7) what they find funny — and why outsiders don't. Then write a 200-word scene where an outsider violates a taboo and the reader understands the stakes without a single line of exposition.
```

### 29. Geography & Travel Logic
```
Act as a map-and-scale consultant. My story spans [LOCATIONS]. Build a travel and geography reference: (1) the distance table between key locations, (2) travel times by [TRANSPORT] — with the variables (weather, season, load) that change the number, (3) the seasons at story time — where each location is when the story happens, (4) how geography shapes conflict and trade — the mountain pass someone controls, the river someone taxes, and (5) the impossibility audit: flag every place my plot timeline is geographically impossible (character in A on day 3, B on day 4) — list each with the fix options: move the event, change the transport, or add travel time to the story.
```

### 30. Fiction Economics
```
You are an economics-of-fiction expert. My world's economy runs on [DESCRIBE]. Follow the money: (1) who produces what — the regions and their outputs, (2) who trades what with whom — the flows, and the dependency that creates leverage, (3) who taxes — and what the tax funds, (4) where wealth concentrates — and why the rich are rich (the mechanism, not just "they own the mines"), and (5) the scarcity that drives the central conflict — name it, or note that I need one. Then: how my villain's plan exploits this economy, and the one plot hole the economy exposes (e.g., "if grain is that scarce, the army starves before chapter 20"). End with the 3 daily-life details the economy implies — prices a reader would hear.
```

## Nonfiction & Professional Writing (Prompts 31–40)

### 31. Book Proposal Skeleton
```
You are a book proposal consultant who has sold nonfiction to major houses. I'm writing a nonfiction book about [TOPIC] for [AUDIENCE]. Draft the proposal skeleton: (1) the hook — 3 options, each one sentence a browser would repeat to a friend, (2) the market need — who has this problem and what they buy now instead, (3) 3 competing titles with the differentiation line for each (my book exists because these don't...), (4) author platform assessment — what I have ([CREDENTIALS/AUDIENCE]) and what to build before submission, (5) the annotated table of contents — 2-line pitch per chapter showing the argument's spine, and (6) the sample chapter opening — 500 words that sound like the book, not like a proposal.
```

### 32. Memoir Structure
```
Act as a memoir structure coach. My life events: [PASTE 10–15 BULLETS]. Find the story: (1) the through-line question — the tension that makes a stranger care (memoir needs a question, not a chronology), (2) where to open — NOT at the beginning: the scene that poses the through-line question, (3) the scene-to-summary ratio per section (memoir dies in summary), (4) what to omit even though it happened — 2 real events that don't serve the through-line, and why leaving them out is honest, not false, (5) the reflection cadence — where the narrator steps back, and (6) the 12-chapter structure: one line per chapter. End with the memoir's promise to the reader stated in one sentence.
```

### 33. Ghostwriting from Raw Material
```
You are a ghostwriting editor who preserves the author's voice. Interview-free, shape this raw material into a bylined article: [PASTE NOTES/TRANSCRIPT]. Rules: (1) keep the author's actual phrases — mark them [KEPT] so I see what's authentically theirs, (2) organize by argument, not chronology — build the spine first, (3) add transitions that sound like the author (match their rhythm), (4) hold a 1,200-word limit — cut the weakest argument, not the last one, and (5) flag the 3 places where I need a quote or fact from the author with [NEEDS: description] — never fabricate. The test: the author should read it and say "that's how I'd say it if I had the time."
```

### 34. The Speech
```
Act as a speechwriter for [OCCASION]. Write a [LENGTH]-minute speech for [AUDIENCE] with core message [MESSAGE]. Structure: (1) a cold-open hook — a scene, a stat, or a question; no "thank you for having me" throat-clearing, (2) 3 pillars, each carried by ONE story (specific people, moments, stakes — not abstract illustration), (3) the callback ending that returns to the opening image and lands the message, (4) stage directions in brackets — (pause), (slow down), (look left) — where they matter, and (5) the contingency cut list: what to drop if we run long, in order — marked [CUT 1], [CUT 2]. Conversational register, ~150 words per minute. End with the one sentence the audience should remember.
```

### 35. Personal Essay Structure
```
You are a personal essay mentor. My essay topic: [TOPIC + PERSONAL ANGLE]. Structure it: (1) the opening scene that embodies the tension — in medias res, present tense, specific, (2) the braided structure — narrative and reflection alternating (say where each turn falls), (3) the turn — the moment understanding changes, planted early so it feels earned, and (4) the ending that answers "so what" without moralizing — connect the personal to the universal, then stop. Draft 600 words: the opening scene in first-person present tense plus the first reflection beat. No life lessons stated outright — the reader draws them or they don't land.
```

### 36. Business Book Pressure Test
```
Act as a business book acquisitions editor who has killed a hundred weak proposals. My book's big idea: [IDEA]. Pressure-test it: (1) what's actually NEW — the sentence that isn't in the 20 other books on this shelf; if there isn't one, say so, (2) the one-sentence takeaway — the thing a reader applies Monday morning, (3) which chapter the book is REALLY about (there's always one — the book is 200 pages of setup for it), (4) what a hostile reviewer would attack — the 3 objections, and my preemptive answers, and (5) the case study that would make it undeniable — real, specific, with numbers. Then restructure the outline around the strongest chapter instead of burying it.
```

### 37. Freelance Writing Career Plan
```
You are a freelance writing career advisor. I write [TYPE — e.g., features, copy, technical] and want to reach $[INCOME GOAL]/year. Build the plan: (1) positioning — the niche intersection where I can be the obvious choice ([INTERESTS] × [EXPERIENCE] × [MARKET THAT PAYS]), (2) 3 portfolio pieces to create — each with specs: topic, length, format, what it proves to a client, (3) 10 pitch targets — publication TYPES with realistic rates for each tier, (4) the pitch email template — under 150 words, proof of understanding their audience in line 1, and (5) the rate card: 3 tiers (per-word, per-project, retainer) with the numbers for [MARKET] — and the rule for when to raise each. Include the income math: clients per month × average fee to hit the goal.
```

### 38. The Op-Ed
```
Act as an op-ed writer with placements in major papers. Turn my expertise on [TOPIC] into an 800-word op-ed for [PUBLICATION TYPE]: (1) the newspeg — why this week (if none exists, the peg is a counterpoint to the prevailing conversation — name it), (2) thesis by paragraph 2 — one arguable sentence, (3) evidence with numbers — 3 proofs, at least one surprising, (4) the counterargument addressed honestly — steelman it, then beat the steelman, (5) the prescriptive ending — what should happen, and who should do it. Voice: authoritative but human. Banned: both-sides hedging, "in today's world," rhetorical questions, the word "delve." End with the 3 publications to pitch first for [TOPIC] and the submission angle for each.
```

### 39. Self-Help Structure Edit
```
You are a developmental editor for self-help. My manuscript teaches [METHOD]. Edit the structure against how people actually change: (1) does each chapter END with a practice — the exercise the reader does, not just reads? list the chapters that fail this, (2) is there a progression of difficulty — chapter 1's practice should be easier than chapter 10's; check the ramp, (3) where does theory outweigh practice — the chapters that need 30% more "do this now," (4) the missing quick-win plan — a 7-day sequence that delivers a visible result (name the day-1 action), and (5) the 12-item revision checklist, ordered by impact on reader transformation. The test: can a reader apply chapter 1 without reading chapter 2? If not, fix that first.
```

### 40. Technical Translation
```
Act as a technical-to-accessible translator. Rewrite this technical explanation for [AUDIENCE]: [PASTE TECHNICAL TEXT]. Rules: (1) no jargon without an inline definition in the same sentence, (2) one idea per paragraph, (3) a concrete analogy within the first 3 sentences — the analogy that maps correctly (say where it breaks down), (4) keep it under [WORD COUNT], and (5) after the rewrite, list what you simplified — each technical truth, the simplification, and the accuracy tradeoff (what a technically-informed reader would object to). If a simplification would mislead on anything that matters for [AUDIENCE]'s decisions, don't make it — find a third option.
```

## Editing, Publishing & Career (Prompts 41–50)

### 41. The 3-Pass Revision Plan
```
You are a revision strategist. My draft is [WORD COUNT] words and I know it has problems but can't see them. Give me a 3-pass plan: Pass 1 (structure) — what to cut, merge, reorder: the 5 diagnostic questions ("which scenes could swap order and no one notices?" = cut candidates), and the time-box. Pass 2 (scene) — stakes, emotion, character logic: its 5 questions. Pass 3 (line) — voice, economy, filter words: its 5 questions. Order the passes so earlier passes can't be skipped (fixing line-level on a scene you'll cut is waste). Give the total calendar for my [WORD COUNT] draft at [HOURS/WEEK] — with the permission note: one pass done well beats three rushed.
```

### 42. First Page Doctor
```
Act as a first-page doctor. Here is my opening: [PASTE FIRST 500 WORDS]. Evaluate against what agents check in the first page: (1) character — is someone specific on the page, or a camera? (2) voice — could this opening belong to another book? then it's nobody's, (3) problem — what's wrong, and do I feel the tension or just hear about it? (4) world clarity — can I picture where I am without info-dump, (5) promise — what kind of story does this page vow? Then: cut anything before the first intriguing line — show the salvage. Offer 3 alternative first lines pulled from LATER in my draft (the best openings are usually buried). Recommend the one for [GENRE].
```

### 43. Query Letter
```
You are a query letter specialist with a client list of deal-makers. Write a query for my novel: [TITLE, GENRE, WORD COUNT] about [PITCH IN 2–3 SENTENCES]. Structure: (1) the personalized opening to [AGENT] — one line proving I know their list (never "I saw you represent books"), (2) the hook: character + choice + stakes in the first 2 sentences — name what the protagonist must do and what happens if they fail, (3) the body: 2–3 paragraphs that read like jacket copy — sell the story, don't summarize it, (4) the bio line: relevant credits or the honest one-liner if none, (5) closing: [WORD COUNT], [GENRE], complete, thanks. 250 words MAX. Banned: rhetorical questions, theme statements, "fiction novel," comping to mega-bestsellers.
```

### 44. Publishing Path Decision
```
Act as a publishing pathway advisor. My book: [GENRE, WORD COUNT, STATUS]. Compare traditional publishing, small press, and self-publishing for my goals [GOALS + TIMELINE]: (1) a decision table — realistic timeline to publication, income mechanics (advance vs. royalties vs. per-unit), creative control, and prestige per path, (2) the honest math for each: what a typical first-book outcome looks like (not the outlier stories), (3) the control tradeoffs — covers, pricing, rights, marketing burden — stated plainly, and (4) my recommendation given [GOALS], with the reasoning, and the next 3 concrete actions for that path — including what to prepare THIS month. If my [GENRE/GOALS] combo strongly favors one path, say it without hedging.
```

### 45. Beta Round Design
```
You are a beta reader coordinator. Design my beta reading round for [BOOK]: (1) how many readers (4–8 for a novel — say why more drowns the signal), (2) the mix: genre fans (do the promises land?), craft readers (does it work?), target audience (do they care?) — with the split, (3) the questionnaire — 10 questions that get honest feedback, not praise ("where did you put it down?" not "did you like it?"), (4) the instruction: mark where you stopped reading for the night — that data is gold, (5) the triage system for contradictory feedback: agreement rule (2+ readers same note = real), the "it's a taste, not a flaw" filter, and who to trust for what kind of note.
```

### 46. The Synopsis
