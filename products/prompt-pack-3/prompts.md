## Strategy & Positioning (Prompts 1–8)

### 1. Business Plan Executive Summary
```
You are a senior strategy consultant who has helped [INDUSTRY] businesses raise over $100M. My company, [COMPANY NAME], sells [PRODUCT/SERVICE] to [TARGET CUSTOMER] and generates $[ANNUAL REVENUE]/year. Write a business plan executive summary covering: the problem (with evidence it's real and expensive), the solution and why now, market size logic, business model with unit economics sketch, competitive edge that a competitor reading this cannot copy, and 12-month milestones with dates. Format: 6 short sections, max 600 words, written to persuade a skeptical investor — every claim either has a number or a plan to get one. No adjectives doing a number's job.
```

### 2. SWOT with Impact Ratings
```
Act as a strategy advisor who treats SWOT as a decision tool, not a wall decoration. Run a structured SWOT for [COMPANY/PRODUCT] competing in [MARKET]. For each quadrant, list 4 specific items (no generics like "strong brand" — say what the strength IS), each with: an impact rating 1–10, a one-line action that exploits (S/O) or mitigates (W/T) it. Then cross the quadrants: my 2 best S-O plays (strength meets opportunity), and the 1 W-T collision that could kill the business if unaddressed. Output as a markdown table, then the 3 strategic priorities for the next 90 days — each with an owner role, a deadline, and a success metric.
```

### 3. Business Model Canvas with Risk Tests
```
You are a business model expert in the Lean Startup tradition. Build a complete Business Model Canvas for [BUSINESS IDEA] targeting [AUDIENCE]. Fill all 9 blocks with 2–4 concrete bullets each — specific enough that someone could disagree with any bullet. Then stress-test it: the 2 riskiest assumptions in the canvas (the ones that, if wrong, collapse the model), each with a 2-week validation experiment costing under $[AMOUNT], what result would confirm the assumption, and what result would kill it. End by naming the canvas block most founders over-optimize while their real risk hides in a different block.
```

### 4. Value Proposition, Three Ways
```
Act as a positioning consultant for [COMPANY NAME], a [BUSINESS TYPE] in [CITY/MARKET]. Write value propositions using this formula: "For [TARGET CUSTOMER] who [NEED], we are the [CATEGORY] that [KEY BENEFIT], unlike [TOP COMPETITOR]." Give 3 variations: one benefit-led, one mechanism-led (how we deliver it), one belief-led (the way we see the world). For each: the customer reaction it's designed to trigger and the marketing surface it fits best (homepage hero, sales call opener, ad). Then recommend the strongest, explain why in 3 bullet points, and give the 2-sentence version for elevator use.
```

### 5. Blue Ocean ERRC Grid
```
You are a Blue Ocean Strategy practitioner. My [BUSINESS TYPE] competes in a crowded [MARKET] against [TOP 3 COMPETITORS]. Apply the Four Actions Framework: ELIMINATE (which industry factors everyone offers that [CUSTOMER] secretly doesn't value), REDUCE (which to strip below industry standard), RAISE (which to push well above), CREATE (which uncontested factors to invent). Present as an ERRC grid with the reasoning for each cell. Then draft the new market positioning statement that follows from the grid — who it serves, what it delivers, what it deliberately doesn't. Flag honestly if my market has no real blue ocean and differentiation is the better play.
```

### 6. Competitive Gap Analysis
```
Act as a competitive intelligence analyst. Compare [MY COMPANY] against [COMPETITOR 1] and [COMPETITOR 2] across: pricing and packaging, target customer, key features and their strengths, marketing channels, and brand voice. Output a comparison table, then: (1) the 3 gaps I can exploit — where they're structurally weak and I can be strong (a gap needs a reason it persists), (2) the 2 threats I must defend against in the next 12 months with the early-warning signal for each, and (3) the one competitor move that would hurt most and my pre-planned response. Base every gap/threat on the comparison, not on vibes.
```

### 7. Plateau Turnaround Diagnosis
```
You are a growth strategy advisor for a [BUSINESS TYPE] that has plateaued at $[REVENUE]/year for [PERIOD]. Diagnose the 5 most likely causes of stagnation for this stage and business type — consider offer maturity, channel saturation, pricing drift, delivery bottleneck, and founder attention. Rank by probability given my context: [ANY CONTEXT — e.g., same revenue 18 months, rising costs, flat traffic]. For each cause: the confirming evidence I should already have in my numbers, and a turnaround initiative with owner, timeline, and success metric. End with the cause you'd bet on if you had to pick one, and why.
```

### 8. Market Entry Plan
```
Act as a market entry consultant. A [BUSINESS TYPE] wants to expand from [COUNTRY/REGION] into [NEW MARKET]. Build a market entry plan covering: (1) localization — what must actually change (pricing, positioning, support language, payment methods) vs. what stays, (2) pricing adjustment logic for local purchasing power and competitors, (3) regulatory considerations for [INDUSTRY] with a "verify with local counsel" flag on anything uncertain, (4) channel partners: the 2 partner types that accelerate entry and the deal structure to offer, and (5) a phased 6-month rollout with milestones and a go/no-go checkpoint at each phase gate — with the specific kill criteria.
```

## Market Research & Customer Insight (Prompts 9–16)

### 9. Market Sizing with Shown Work
```
You are a market research analyst. Size the total addressable market (TAM), serviceable market (SAM), and share obtainable (SOM) for [PRODUCT/SERVICE] aimed at [AUDIENCE] in [GEOGRAPHY]. Show every assumption as a step: population or business count → filtered by [RELEVANT CRITERIA] → willingness to pay → frequency. State the final numbers in a summary table (bottom-up and top-down, side by side). Then stress-test it: the 2 assumptions that most swing the number, what a skeptical investor would challenge first, and what evidence would settle the challenge. Never round up to sound impressive — show the honest range.
```

### 10. Three-Tier Persona Cards
```
Act as a customer insight researcher. Create 3 detailed buyer personas for [PRODUCT/SERVICE]: a budget buyer, a mid-market buyer, and a premium buyer — all real in this market, not strawmen. For each persona card: demographics and role, the 3 daily pain points in their own words, buying triggers (what event makes them start looking), objections in order of frequency, preferred channels where they actually spend attention, the quote that captures how they'd describe their problem at a bar, and what they'd pay for a perfect solution. End with: which persona my current offer actually fits best, and the mismatch that explains any recent lost sales.
```

### 11. Trend Radar with Actions
```
You are a trend analyst for the [INDUSTRY] sector. Identify the 5 trends most likely to affect a [BUSINESS TYPE] over the next 24 months — span technology, buyer behavior, regulation, channel shifts, and business models. For each trend: the evidence it's real (not a prediction — what's already visible), the impact rating (high/medium/low) with a one-line why, the impact on MY business specifically (threat, opportunity, or neutral-with-conditions), and the one action to take now, with the cost of acting early vs. the risk of waiting. Close with the trend most [INDUSTRY] businesses are ignoring that will matter most.
```

### 12. Survey That Answers Decisions
```
Act as a survey design expert. Write a 10-question customer survey for [PRODUCT/SERVICE] that measures: satisfaction, unmet needs, and willingness to pay. Mix multiple choice, rating scales, and exactly 2 open questions (place them last — placement matters for completion rate). Write the intro message (why we're asking, how long it takes, what they get). Then map each question to the business decision it informs: which question answers "should we build X," which answers "is our pricing wrong," which answers "who's churning." Kill any question that doesn't map to a decision I'd act on.
```

### 13. Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity
```
You are a pricing researcher. Design a Van Westendorp price sensitivity survey (the 4 standard questions — too cheap, cheap/expensive, expensive, too expensive) plus 2 follow-up questions for [PRODUCT/SERVICE] targeting [AUDIENCE]: one purchase-intent question at the indicated acceptable price, one open "what would make it worth more to you." Write the exact question wording (wording changes results — keep it neutral, no anchoring). Then explain how to read the results: where the acceptable-price range typically intersects for [CATEGORY], the difference between the point of marginal cheapness and the point of marginal expensiveness, and the decision rule for setting price from the output.
```

### 14. Jobs-to-be-Done Canvas
```
Act as a jobs-to-be-done researcher. My product is [PRODUCT]. Map: (1) the core functional job — what customers hire it to do, stated as "when [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]", (2) the emotional and social jobs layered on top (how they want to feel, how they want to be seen), (3) the forces of progress — push of the situation and pull of the new solution, and (4) the anxieties and habits holding them back, each with the counter-message that dissolves it. Present as a one-page JTBD canvas with the job story at the top. Flag where my current marketing message speaks to the functional job but ignores the stronger emotional job.
```

### 15. Voice-of-Customer Theme Analysis
```
You are a voice-of-customer analyst. I will paste [N] customer reviews or support tickets about [PRODUCT]: [PASTE REVIEWS]. Do this: (1) identify the top 5 recurring themes, clustered into praise vs. complaint, with the frequency count of each, (2) quote one representative line per theme — real customer words, not paraphrase, (3) translate each complaint theme into a specific product or process improvement, and (4) identify the theme customers mention that we likely think is fine — the expectation gap. Output as a table: theme | count | praise/complaint | representative quote | improvement action. End with the single change that addresses the most complaint volume at once.
```

### 16. Mystery Shopping Protocol
```
Act as a competitive mystery-shopping planner. Design a step-by-step protocol to evaluate [COMPETITOR]'s sales process: (1) website pass — what to look for (pricing transparency, friction in the demo request, response expectation setting), (2) demo call — the 5 questions to ask that reveal their pitch, weaknesses they avoid, and how they handle objections, (3) email follow-up — what to record (sequence length, tone, speed, personalization level), and (4) the signals sheet: response time, pricing transparency, follow-up cadence, and the "giveaway" behaviors that indicate how their sales team is compensated. Deliver as a fill-in-the-blank field worksheet I could hand to a VA. Stay within ethical bounds — no misrepresentation of identity for regulated industries.
```

Format: workflow descriptions plus a Monday-to-Friday management checklist a busy owner can follow in 30 minutes a day. Flag the workflow most likely to break as we grow past [NUMBER]× headcount.

## Financial Planning & Pricing (Prompts 25–32)

### 25. 12-Month P&L Forecast
```
You are a fractional CFO for a [BUSINESS TYPE] with $[REVENUE] annual revenue. Build a 12-month profit and loss forecast: revenue by stream with the growth assumption per stream, cost of goods sold and gross margin, operating expenses by category (people, tools, marketing, rent/other), and net profit per month. Present as a month-by-month table. Then: (1) highlight the 3 assumptions that most affect the outcome — and show what the year looks like if each is 20% wrong (best/worst case), (2) flag the months where cash is tightest even if profitable, and (3) name the metric to watch monthly that says the forecast is drifting. State clearly which numbers are my inputs vs. your assumptions.
```

### 26. Pricing Model Decision
```
Act as a pricing strategist. My [PRODUCT/SERVICE] costs $[COST] to deliver; competitors charge $[PRICE RANGE]. Evaluate 3 pricing models for my case — flat, tiered, and usage-based — against: revenue predictability, customer fairness perception at different volumes, the cost of administering each, and how each model changes buyer behavior. Recommend one with the reasoning, then design the offer around it: the actual tiers or rates, what's included at each level, and the price presentation copy that justifies the price without discounting — leading with value framing, handling the "why not cheaper" objection in one sentence. End with the discount policy: what's allowed, what's never allowed, and the one exception rule.
```

### 27. Unit Economics Deep-Dive
```

Calculate the customer economics for my [BUSINESS TYPE] with these inputs: CAC $[AMOUNT], average order value $[AMOUNT], gross margin [PERCENT]%, repeat purchase frequency [FREQUENCY], and customer lifespan [LIFESPAN]. Walk through the math step by step: CAC payback period (state in months and orders), LTV with the calculation shown — pick and justify gross-margin LTV vs. revenue LTV, and the LTV:CAC ratio. Then tell me: which lever to improve FIRST (a 10% gain in CAC vs. AOV vs. frequency vs. margin — show which moves LTV:CAC most), the realistic intervention for that lever in 90 days, and the ratio at which I should stop optimizing retention and spend on acquisition instead.
```

### 28. Founder's Financial Pack

### 29. 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast
