## Lesson Planning & Curriculum (Prompts 1–8)

### 1. The Complete Lesson Plan
```
You are an instructional designer with 15 years across K-12 and adult education. Create a complete lesson plan for [SUBJECT] on [TOPIC] for [GRADE LEVEL/ADULT AUDIENCE], lasting [DURATION]. Include: (1) learning objectives with measurable verbs — what learners will do, not "understand", (2) the 3-minute hook/opener with script, (3) direct instruction outline with key points and misconceptions to pre-empt, (4) guided practice — the activity, grouping, and your role during it, (5) independent practice with the success criteria visible to learners, (6) formative assessment — how you'll know before the bell rings, (7) closure that consolidates, not just collects. Differentiate for struggling and advanced learners. Output: a structured plan a substitute could deliver.
```

### 2. Course Architecture From Zero
```
Act as a curriculum architect who designs courses people finish. Design a [LENGTH]-week course on [SUBJECT] taking [AUDIENCE] from [STARTING LEVEL] to [GOAL LEVEL]. Structure: (1) modules with weekly objectives — each objective an observable skill, (2) the project per module that proves mastery — not a quiz, a artifact, (3) prerequisite flow — what must come before what, drawn as a dependency map, (4) the capstone that integrates the whole course. Show the full syllabus in one table: week, module, objective, project, assessment. Include the two weeks where learners historically quit and what you build in to catch them.
```

### 3. Bloom's-Taxonomy Objective Converter
```
You are a learning objectives specialist who audits curriculum for measurable verbs. Convert these vague topics into measurable learning objectives: [PASTE TOPICS]. For each: (1) the appropriate cognitive level on Bloom's taxonomy — remember through create — and why that level fits the audience [AUDIENCE], (2) the verb-matched objective ("analyze", "design", "construct" — never "understand" or "learn"), (3) how it will be assessed — the evidence that matches the verb, (4) the observable behavior that proves achievement — what you'd literally watch a learner do. Output: a table of original → converted, with the assessment column. Flag any objective that claims a level higher than its assessment can evidence.
```

### 4. Backward Design a Unit
```
Act as a backward design coach trained on Wiggins & McTighe's Understanding by Design. Help me plan a unit on [TOPIC] the backward way: (1) define the transfer goal — what students do with this in real life, 5 years later, (2) design the summative assessment FIRST — the authentic performance task, not a chapter test, (3) determine the evidence — the formative checkpoints along the way, (4) then build the learning plan backward — activities chosen only because they produce the evidence. Show the 3-stage template (desired results, evidence, learning plan) filled in for my unit. Include the trap to avoid: activities that are engaging but produce no evidence of the goal.
```

### 5. Course Sequence Dependency Audit
```
You are a course sequencing expert who finds the ordering mistakes that lose learners. My course covers [LIST TOPICS]. Analyze the sequence: (1) conceptual dependencies — which topics must precede which, and where my current order violates them, (2) the wall moments — where learners typically hit cognitive overload, and what to split or scaffold there, (3) interleaving opportunities — which topics to interleave for retention instead of blocking, (4) review checkpoints — where spaced retrieval of earlier material belongs. Propose the revised order with a reasoning table: old position, new position, why. Flag the single riskiest ordering choice I've made.
```

### 6. Differentiation for a Split-Level Class
```
Act as a lesson differentiation planner for real classrooms, not ideal ones. My class has [LEVEL RANGE: e.g., 4th-grade reading levels from 2nd to 8th]. Differentiate this lesson on [TOPIC]: (1) the core objective ALL learners hit — the one non-negotiable, (2) the scaffolds — sentence starters, graphic organizers, vocabulary pre-teach — with which learners get which, and how to hand them out without stigma, (3) the extension for advanced learners — deeper, not just more, (4) the assessment options that measure the same objective through different doors. Output: a tiered lesson plan with the scaffold/extension table, and the classroom-management note for running 3 tiers at once.
```

### 7. Project-Based Learning Unit
```
You are a project-based learning designer whose units end with work students actually show off. Create a PBL unit for [SUBJECT/GRADE] on [TOPIC], duration [WEEKS]: (1) the driving question — open-ended, worth answering, tied to the real world, (2) the authentic final product for a real audience — name the audience and the venue (presentation, publication, exhibition), (3) milestone checkpoints with dates, (4) rubric categories with performance-level descriptors, (5) the workshop mini-lessons to teach along the way — the just-in-time skills, (6) the reflection component that closes the loop. Output: the unit overview, milestone calendar, and rubric. Include what to cut if the unit runs long.
```

### 8. Blended Corporate Training Conversion
```

Decide: (1) what becomes e-learning — the knowledge that scales asynchronously, (2) what must stay live — practice, feedback, and the social learning, (3) what becomes on-the-job — application assignments with manager sign-off, (4) the spacing and reinforcement schedule — how learning survives past day 3, (5) the manager touchpoints — the pre-brief and the 3 coaching moments, (6) how completion ties to performance metrics, not seat time. ## Assessment & Grading (Prompts 9–16)

### 9. The Bloom's-Balanced Assessment
```
You are an assessment design expert who writes items that measure what they claim to. Create a [QUIZ/TEST] on [TOPIC] for [LEVEL] with [NUMBER] questions covering all Bloom's levels: [NUMBER] recall, [NUMBER] comprehension, [NUMBER] application, [NUMBER] analysis. Include: (1) the answer key with the rationale per item, (2) point values weighted to match the levels, (3) 2 bonus questions for critical thinking, (4) a flag on any ambiguous item you catch yourself writing — and its fix. Output: the assessment first, then the key. Rule: no item should be answerable by test-taking savvy alone — eliminate the grammatical clues that leak answers.
```

### 10. The Rubric That Grades Itself
```
Act as a rubric designer whose rubrics students actually use. Create an analytic rubric for [ASSIGNMENT: e.g., persuasive essay, lab report, presentation] with [NUMBER] criteria: [LIST CRITERIA]. Requirements: (1) 4 performance levels — exceeds, meets, approaching, beginning — with observable descriptors: what a grader literally sees, no "good" or "adequate", (2) each cell written so two different graders would agree, (3) point allocations that reflect what actually matters, (4) the student-friendly version that doubles as a checklist students use before submitting. Output: the full rubric table plus the student version. Include the calibration exercise: the sample of student work you'd have graders score together to align.
```

### 11. 15 Formative Assessments Beyond the Quiz
```
You are a formative assessment specialist who checks for understanding every 10 minutes without killing momentum. Give me 15 formative techniques for [SUBJECT] beyond the quiz: exit tickets, hinge questions, cold-call protocols, concept checks, self-assessment tools — and the rest. For each: (1) what it measures — the specific understanding, (2) time cost, (3) when to deploy — the lesson moment it fits, (4) the teacher move it triggers when half the class fails — reteach, regroup, or slow down. Output: a technique table, then the "if-then" response plan: the data pattern → the move. Prioritize the techniques that work with [CLASS SIZE] students in [DURATION] lessons.
```

### 12. Feedback That Students Use
```
Act as a feedback specialist who writes feedback students actually act on. Write effective feedback on this student work: [PASTE WORK]. Follow the protocol: (1) 1 specific strength grounded in the work — quote the exact line, (2) 1–2 high-leverage improvements with the exact location and what to do there, (3) 1 next-step action — the single move that improves the next submission most, (4) the grade last, if applicable, so it doesn't blind the student to the feedback. Then explain the feedback principles you applied — so I can replicate the pattern on the next 30 papers. Output: the feedback itself, then the principles. Constraint: feedback a student reads in under 90 seconds.
```

### 13. Quiz Item Quality Audit
```
You are a question quality auditor who finds the flaws that make test results meaningless. Review these items: [PASTE QUESTIONS]. Check each for: (1) two-arguably-correct answers, (2) unfair reading demand — vocabulary or syntax that measures reading, not the target skill, (3) clueing — grammar or construction that leaks the answer, (4) negative constructions and double negatives, (5) bias — content that advantages some students for irrelevant reasons. Output: a flaw report per item — issue, why it matters, the rewrite. Then the clean versions of all items, ready to deploy. If an item is unfixable, say so and replace it.
```

### 14. Test-Prep Item Writing
```
Act as a test-prep item writer who matches released-exam difficulty distributions. Write [NUMBER] practice questions in [EXAM FORMAT: AP, SAT, MCAT, certification] style for [TOPIC]: (1) the stimulus text/passage where the format requires it — written at authentic exam complexity, (2) 4 answer options with plausible distractors — each distractor wrong for a reason a real student would actually be wrong, (3) the correct answer, (4) the explanation for why each distractor is wrong — the misconception it catches. Match the released-exam difficulty distribution and say what yours is. Output: stimulus, items, key, distractor analysis. Flag where my [TOPIC] coverage is thinner than the exam's blueprint.
```

### 15. Mastery-Based Retake Protocol
```
You are a grade-recovery and mastery specialist who believes a grade should mean the same thing in June as in September. My student failed [ASSESSMENT] on [TOPIC]. Design the mastery-based retake protocol: (1) the re-learning path — targeted mini-lessons for each missed concept, matched to the item analysis, (2) the practice cycle — spaced, not crammed, with self-check keys, (3) the alternative assessment — different evidence, same standard, (4) the reflection requirement — what the student learns about their own learning. Keep it fair: same standard, different evidence — and keep it sustainable: design it once, apply to every retake. Output: the protocol, the re-learning assignments, and the conversation script that frames retakes as normal, not remedial.
```

### 16. Data-Driven Reteach Plan
```
Act as a data-driven instruction coach who turns item analysis into next week's plan. My class scored: [PASTE SCORE DATA BY QUESTION/STANDARD]. Analyze: (1) the 3 most-missed concepts — separated from the most-missed questions, (2) the error types — conceptual (wrong understanding), procedural (right idea, wrong execution), or careless (right idea, wrong transcription) — with the tell for each, (3) the reteach plan for the whole class — 20 minutes, one concept, different method than first teaching, (4) the small-group plan for the students who need more, (5) the re-assessment timeline and format. Output: analysis table, reteach plan, small-group plan, calendar. Include the standard you will NOT reteach — the one the data says is fine.
```
