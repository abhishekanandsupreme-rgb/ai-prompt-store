## Idea Validation (Prompts 1–8)

### 1. The Idea Stress-Test
```
You are a startup validation expert who has reviewed 1,000+ early-stage ideas and specialize in killing bad ones fast. My idea: [IDEA] for [AUDIENCE] who [PAIN POINT]. Stress-test it: (1) rank the 5 riskiest assumptions by uncertainty, (2) design the cheapest test for each — under $100 and under 1 week, (3) define kill criteria — the exact result that should make me stop, and (4) name the one assumption that, if wrong, invalidates everything downstream. Output: a ranked assumptions table (assumption, why risky, test, cost, kill criterion), then a one-paragraph honest verdict. Be the friend who saves me 6 months, not the one who cheers.
```

### 2. The Mom Test Interview Script
```
Act as a customer development coach trained on The Mom Test. I'm about to talk to potential customers about [PROBLEM], and my current questions are naive: [PASTE QUESTIONS]. (1) Diagnose each question — does it invite compliments, hypotheticals, or pitch feedback instead of facts? (2) Rewrite each into a learning conversation about actual past behavior. (3) Then build the 10-question interview script that surfaces how they really solve [PROBLEM] today: what they tried, what they paid, what they gave up on, and the moment the pain actually bites. Include instructions for what to note verbatim, and 3 questions to ask about their wallet, not their opinion.
```

### 3. Why-Now Market Timing Analysis
```
You are a market timing analyst who studies why startups succeed or fail on timing alone. My idea [IDEA] depends on [SHIFT/BEHAVIOR CHANGE]. Assess the timing honestly: (1) the enabling change — technology, regulation, cost curve, or behavior — and whether it's actually here or still coming, (2) where we sit on the adoption curve and who the early adopters really are, (3) the competitors who tried this before and failed — and what specifically has changed since, (4) whether waiting 2 years would make this easier, cheaper, or dead. End with a timing verdict (early, right, late, wrong) and the reasoning. Verdict with evidence, not encouragement.
```

### 4. The Startup Pre-Mortem
```
Act as a pre-mortem facilitator running the exercise with a founding team. My startup [IDEA] launches in [MONTHS]. Imagine it's 12 months from now and the venture has failed. (1) Write the top 10 plausible causes of death specific to my idea — not generic startup killers, the ones that fit my market, model, and team. (2) Cluster them into four buckets: market, product, founder, execution. (3) For the 3 most likely causes, give the prevention plan: what to do in the next 90 days, the leading indicator to watch, and the trip-wire that means act now. Output: the failure story first (2 paragraphs), then the causes table, then prevention plans.
```

### 5. TAM Reality Check
```
You are a TAM reality-check advisor who has sat across from hundreds of investors debunking market-size slides. My investor deck claims [MARKET SIZE] for [IDEA]. Red-team the number: (1) show the top-down vs. bottom-up gap and where my top-down number inflates, (2) separate market size from gettable revenue — who actually has the budget and urgency, (3) build the honest bottom-up calculation for my case: reachable customers × realistic price × win rate, with each assumption stated. Then tell me: the defensible number to put in the deck, how to frame it so it survives diligence, and the one sentence that makes an investor trust my market sizing more than the founders who claim bigger numbers.
```

### 6. Competitive Moat Assessment
```
Act as a competitive moat assessor in the style of a strategy partner at a top fund. My [BUSINESS] has these advantages: [ADVANTAGES]. Evaluate each against the real moat categories — network effects, switching costs, brand, scale economics, embedded workflows, regulation, proprietary data. For each advantage: is it a durable defensibility, a temporary edge, or a commodity feature competitors copy in one sprint? (1) Score each 1–10 for defensibility with reasoning. (2) Recommend how to deepen the 2 strongest moats in the next 2 quarters. (3) Name the single competitor move that would hurt me most, how likely it is, and my pre-emptive response.
```

### 7. The Pivot-or-Persevere Coach
```
You are a pivot coach who has guided founders through both fatal stubbornness and fatal thrashing. My [STARTUP] built [A] and has [TRACTION SIGNALS FOR A], but users keep asking for [B]. Assess the pivot evidence honestly: (1) the accidental-discovery pattern — how many serious B requests, from whom, and what they'd pay, (2) the energy signal — where the team and I naturally do better work, (3) the economics — revenue logic, sales cycle, and margin of B vs. A, (4) the sunk-cost trap — which of my reasons to persevere are actually just emotional. Then design the 30-day pivot test that validates B without abandoning A: the smallest commitment that produces real evidence. End with a persevere/pivot/wait recommendation and what would change it.
```

### 8. Filtered Business Idea Generator
```
Act as a business idea generator with a brutal filter. My inputs — skills: [SKILLS], resources: [RESOURCES], interests: [INTERESTS], network: [NETWORK]. (1) Generate 10 business ideas at the intersection, each with a one-line model of how money changes hands. (2) Then kill 7 of them against this filter: unsexy problem people already pay to solve, proven willingness to pay (not "would use"), founder advantage — why me specifically, and a reachable path to the first 10 customers. (3) Show the 3 survivors with the wedge strategy for each: the narrow first offer, the first-customer channel, and the expansion path after the wedge works. Be willing to kill all 10 and say what input is the bottleneck.
```

## MVP & Product (Prompts 9–16)

### 9. Strip It to the MVP
```
You are an MVP coach who has shipped 50+ products and knows the difference between an MVP and a small version of everything. My full product vision: [DESCRIBE VISION]. (1) Name the single core job users hire this for — the one sentence a user would say: "I use it to ___". (2) List the 3 features that deliver that job and nothing more. (3) List everything you're cutting, with one line each on why it's cuttable. (4) Design the manual-first "Wizard of Oz" version I could run next week with humans doing the backend work — and what running it manually teaches that code can't. (5) Define the one success metric that proves demand. Output: a one-page build/not-build table plus the manual-first plan.
```

### 10. The 2-Page PRD
```
Act as a product requirements writer whose PRDs get built correctly without meetings. Write the PRD for [FEATURE]: (1) problem statement with evidence — where the signal came from, (2) target user and their context, (3) user stories with acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then form, (4) explicit out-of-scope list so scope creep has nowhere to hide, (5) success metrics — what number moves and by how much, (6) rollout plan including the kill switch. Constraint: 2 pages max, written so a developer who has never spoken to me could build it. No marketing language, no "seamless" or "robust" — verbs and specifics only.
```

### 11. Onboarding Funnel Design
```
You are a user onboarding designer who measures success by time-to-value, not signups. My [PRODUCT] activates when the user completes [ACTION]. Design the onboarding funnel: (1) the aha-moment path — the minimum steps from landing to [ACTION], with each step justified or killed, (2) the friction inventory — what to cut now, delay until after activation, or make optional forever, (3) progressive profiling — which data to ask for at which stage and why asking early kills conversion, (4) the 3 intervention emails for users who stall at each major step — write the actual copy, under 60 words each. Output: the funnel diagram as text, the friction table, and the email drafts.
```

### 12. Product Analytics Instrumentation Plan
```
Act as a product analytics coach who has instrumented products from zero to millions of events. My MVP gets [SIGNUPS] per month but only [PERCENT]% activate. Build my instrumentation plan: (1) the 5 events that matter and why, (2) the funnel definition for [PRODUCT] — event names, order, and the properties to capture on each, (3) the activation metric — the exact formula, (4) the cohort view I should actually check weekly and how to read it, (5) the 3 highest-value hypotheses for the biggest leak and what data would confirm each. Critically: list what NOT to instrument yet — events that create noise before product-market fit. Output: an event schema table, then the weekly dashboard spec.
```

### 13. Product-Market Fit Diagnosis
```
You are a product-market fit diagnostician who tells founders the truth their retention curve already knows. My signals: [RETENTION DATA, GROWTH RATE, ENGAGEMENT DATA]. Diagnose honestly: (1) the retention curve shape — flattening at what level (PMF signal) vs. decaying to zero (no PMF), (2) the Sean Ellis test framing — what % "very disappointed" threshold means and how to run the survey properly, (3) cohort analysis — are newer cohorts retaining better, same, or worse, and what that implies, (4) the 30/100 metrics that matter at my stage. Then the verdict: pre-PMF, PMF emerging, or PMF — and the single next move each verdict demands. Do not soften a bad read.
```

### 14. RICE Prioritization With a Strategy Check
```
Act as a feature prioritization specialist who has run this exercise with 100 product teams. My backlog: [PASTE 15 FEATURES]. (1) Score each with RICE — reach, impact, confidence, effort — showing the math, not just the score. (2) Sanity-check the winner against strategy: does it actually serve [STRATEGIC GOAL], or does it just score well? (3) Identify the top 3 for the next sprint and what a realistic sprint-sized slice of each looks like. (4) Name the 3 backlog items to explicitly kill — the zombies that haunt every planning meeting — and the one-line death certificate for each. Output: the full scoring table sorted by RICE, then the sprint recommendation, then the kill list.
```

### 15. No-Code MVP Stack Plan
```
You are a no-code MVP advisor who has shipped validation products in single weekends. I have [BUDGET] and [TIMEFRAME] to validate [IDEA] — the hypothesis to test is [HYPOTHESIS]. Recommend the stack: (1) the tool for [CORE FUNCTION] and why it beats alternatives for this specific use, (2) the duct-tape versions of expensive parts — forms, sheets, manual fulfillment, scheduled emails, (3) the total cost breakdown with monthly run-rate after launch, (4) what I should NOT build at all — where [MANUAL PROCESS] done by a human beats software for the first 50 users. Finish with the 1-week build plan: day-by-day milestones ending in "live and recruiting test users". Flag anything that will need rewriting in real code later.
```

### 16. Competitor Product Teardown
```
Act as a product teardown expert who reverse-engineers winning products for a living. Analyze [COMPETITOR PRODUCT]'s end-to-end product experience: (1) their onboarding choices — what they ask for, in what order, what they deliberately defer, (2) the habit hook — the trigger-action-reward loop that brings users back, (3) their paywall placement and messaging — what's free, when they ask for money, and the psychology of that moment, (4) what they deliberately don't do — the feature omissions that signal their strategy, (5) the 3 genuine weaknesses or gaps in their flow that my product can exploit. Output: a flow analysis by section, then the 3 exploitable openings ranked by how hard they are for the incumbent to close.
```

## Customers & Sales (Prompts 17–24)

