## Planning & Prioritization (Prompts 1–8)

### 1. The Weekly Plan from Chaos
```
You are an executive productivity coach who has run this exercise with hundreds of overloaded professionals. My role: [ROLE]. Here is my current task list: [PASTE TASKS]. Sort everything into a weekly plan using the Eisenhower matrix, with a realistic effort estimate per task and the day you'd place it. Then: (1) identify the 3 tasks that create 80% of my outcomes — defend why each made the cut, (2) what to delegate with a 2-line handoff note for each, and (3) what to drop entirely — with the reason and the one sentence I can tell whoever asked for it. End with Monday's first 90 minutes, scheduled concretely.
```

### 2. Paralysis Triage
```
Act as a priority surgeon. My to-do list has [NUMBER] items and I feel paralyzed: [PASTE LIST]. Apply ruthless triage: (1) which 3 items will matter in 90 days — trace each to an outcome it serves or cut it, (2) which are busywork disguised as progress (organizing, optimizing, tool-configuring — the productive procrastination patterns), (3) which are someone else's priority living on my list — say whose. Then give me today's 3-item plan with time estimates that fit a realistic day, plus permission language for declining the rest — one sentence per dropped item, polite and final. No guilt framing: the goal is relief, not a better-organized prison.
```

### 3. The 90-Day Plan
```
You are a quarterly planning facilitator. Run me through a 90-day planning session for my goal: [GOAL]. Structure: (1) review of last quarter — wins, misses, and the honest reason for each, (2) choose the single most important objective — I'll push back if you let me choose 3, so force the ranking logic, (3) break it into monthly milestones with the success criterion per month, (4) weekly lead measures — the inputs I control, not outcomes I await, and (5) the definition of done: what "achieved" looks like in observable terms. Output as a one-page plan I can print: sections, not paragraphs. Include the weekly 15-minute check-in question set.
```

### 4. WOOP Goal Design
```
Act as a goal-setting expert using the WOOP method (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) — the research-backed version of positive thinking. My wish: [GOAL]. Walk me through: (1) the Wish — restated as specific and challenging but achievable, (2) the Outcome — the best result, visualized concretely: what I'd see, feel, and have, (3) the Obstacle — force it INTERNAL: a behavior or belief of mine, not circumstances (the classic failure is blaming time when the obstacle is avoidance), name mine precisely, and (4) the Plan — if-then implementation intentions for the 2 most likely failure points, worded "If [obstacle appears], then I will [specific action]." End with the 10-word version I can memorize.
```

### 5. OKRs for a Small Team
```
You are an OKR coach for teams under 20 people — where enterprise OKR theater dies. Help me write OKRs for [TEAM/PERSON] for [QUARTER]: (1) one objective that's inspiring and qualitative — a rallying cry, not a metric, (2) 3 key results that are numeric and time-bound — each with the baseline, the target, and the source of the number, (3) the NOT-doing list: what we're explicitly deprioritizing this quarter, written as commitments, and (4) the weekly check-in ritual: 3 questions, 15 minutes, no scoring theater. Then the honesty check: which key result is actually uncontrollable (depends on the market, not us) and needs rewording to measure our action instead.
```

### 6. Decision Framework, Weighted
```
Act as a decision-making specialist. I'm torn between [OPTION A] and [OPTION B] for [DECISION CONTEXT]. Build my decision process: (1) the criteria that matter — 5 max, drawn from what I actually care about, not what sounds rational, (2) weight them (must sum to 100) and score both options 1–10 per criterion — show the table and the totals, (3) the sensitivity check: which criterion, if re-weighted, flips the result — that's where my real values live, (4) reversible vs. irreversible — if reversible: the cheap, fast test that beats analysis (design the 1-week experiment), and (5) what would change my mind — the future evidence that should reopen this decision, written down now so I notice it later.
```

### 7. Capacity Rebuild
```
You are a capacity planning advisor. My calendar is oversubscribed: [DESCRIBE ROLES AND COMMITMENTS]. Calculate my real available hours: 168 per week minus sleep ([HOURS]), minus [COMMUTE/CHORES/FAMILY — LIST], minus the invisible load (decisions, transitions — estimate honestly). Then rebuild a sustainable week: (1) focus blocks — protected, named by the work they hold, (2) meeting windows — clustered, not sprinkled, (3) the admin batch — one slot, not a daily drip, and (4) buffers — 20% unallocated, because weeks never go to plan. Show what must be renegotiated and with whom — the specific conversation and the opening line for each. End with the canary: the signal that I'm oversubscribed again, and the monthly check.
```

### 8. Project Pileup Triage
```
Act as a project triage expert. I have these projects running: [PASTE PROJECTS]. Assess each on: energy level (drains or fills me), deadline (real or self-imposed — mark which), financial impact, and dependency (who's waiting on me, who I'm waiting on). Then: (1) sequence the survivors — the order that finishes the most projects fastest, (2) kill or pause the ones that don't survive scrutiny — with the one-sentence closing message per stakeholder, and (3) the one-project-at-a-time system that prevents this pileup recurring: the WIP limit, the intake rule (no new project until X), and the exception criteria — because life will test it. Include the 15-minute weekly review that catches the next pileup at 2 projects, not 6.
```

## Time Management Systems (Prompts 9–18)

### 9. The Time-Block Template
```
You are a time-blocking architect. Build my ideal week as a time-block template: work hours [WORK HOURS], roles [ROLES], recurring commitments [RECURRING COMMITMENTS]. Include: 2 daily deep-work blocks of 90 minutes (placed at my peak — I'm a [MORNING/EVENING] person), meeting clusters (batched into 2 windows, not scattered), one admin batch, a shutdown ritual slot, and personal time marked as unmovable — schedule it first, like a flight. Present as a Monday-to-Sunday grid table. Then the 3 rules for when the day collapses: which blocks move, which die, and the one that survives any day — the 25-minute minimum version of my deep work.
```

### 10. Deep Work Protocol
```
Act as a deep work coach. My focus is destroyed by [INTERRUPTIONS — e.g., Slack, notifications, open-door colleagues, my own phone]. Design my deep work protocol: (1) the environment setup checklist — the 5-minute pre-session ritual: notifications, tabs, phone placement, desk state, (2) the startup ritual that triggers flow — the same 3 actions every session, so the cue becomes automatic, (3) the session structure: [WORK/BREAK RATIO — e.g., 50/10 or 90/15] with break activities that restore (walk, water, stretch — NOT the phone: scrolling resets the focus cost), (4) the distraction parking lot — a notepad system for the "but I need to check" impulses, and (5) the 2-week ramp plan to reach 3 hours of focused work per day, starting embarrassingly small in week 1.
```

### 11. Calendar Audit
```

### 12. Meeting Reduction Plan
```
Act as a meeting reducer. My week has [NUMBER] meetings: [LIST THEM]. Apply the decision framework to each: CANCEL (async update works), SHORTEN (25-minute default — parkinson's law applies), BATCH (which ones cluster), or KEEP (the real-time value ones). For every canceled meeting, draft the async alternative: the Loom outline (what to cover in 3 minutes), the doc, or the Slack thread — with the template so it's not more work than the meeting. For shortened ones: the tightened agenda. Target: cut total meeting hours by 40%. Include the decline script for future invites that don't state a purpose or decision — one polite sentence with the alternative offer.
```

### 13. Energy-Based Day Design
```
You are an energy management specialist. My energy peaks at [TIMES] and crashes at [TIMES]. Design my day around energy, not hours: (1) creative work at peak — placed in the calendar by name, (2) admin at the trough — email, expenses, the tasks that need presence but not intelligence, (3) social tasks at the second peak — meetings and calls when I have people-energy, (4) the recovery protocol for the crash — the 20-minute version (walk, not phone) and the 5-minute version (when the crash hits mid-meeting), and (5) the 5-minute energy reset between blocks: the exact sequence (stand, breathe, water, one-sentence intention). Then the honesty check: what my current schedule gets backwards — name the specific mismatch.
```

### 14. Focus Session System

### 15. Morning Routine, Real

### 16. The Shutdown Ritual
```
Act as a shutdown ritual specialist. My work bleeds into evenings: [DESCRIBE SITUATION]. Design my end-of-day shutdown: (1) capture — the loose tasks in my head onto tomorrow's list (the Zeigarnik effect: unrecorded tasks ping all night), (2) review tomorrow's 3 priorities — chosen now so the morning doesn't start with a decision, (3) the "I'm done" signal — the physical action that tells my brain work is closed (close the laptop lid + say it aloud + a specific playlist or walk — the exact sequence), and (4) the boundary phrase for after-hours pings — the response I send at 8pm that respects the sender and me: "I'll pick this up tomorrow at [TIME]." Max 15 minutes total. Include what to do when the ritual fails and I'm back online at 10pm anyway.
```

### 17. Context-Switching Cost Analysis
```
You are a context-switching cost analyst. My days are shredded by switching between [PROJECTS/TOOLS]. Calculate: (1) my switching count on a typical day (estimate from my description), (2) the cost — research puts a switch at 10–23 minutes of re-entry time; show my daily and weekly loss in hours, (3) then design the batching system: task-type days or half-days (map which — e.g., Mondays = writing, Tuesdays = meetings), the single intake point (everything enters one place, not five), and (4) the WIP limit: max 3 open projects, with the rules for exceptions (the only valid reason to exceed: a genuine emergency — define it). End with the one switch I can eliminate this week for the biggest immediate recovery.
```

## Task Execution & Follow-Through (Prompts 19–28)

### 19. Procrastination Diagnosis
```
You are an anti-procrastination specialist who knows avoidance is information. I've been avoiding [TASK] for [DURATION]. Diagnose which of the 5 causes it is: (1) unclear next action — the task is actually a project in disguise, (2) perfectionism — I can't start because I can't start perfectly, (3) fear of judgment — whose, and of what, (4) task too big — my brain correctly refuses the undifferentiated blob, (5) low meaning — some part of me doesn't believe it matters. Match the diagnosis to my specific case — say which and why. Then the matching intervention, plus the exact 10-minute starting action I take today: concrete, embarrassingly small, ending mid-task (the Zeigarnik hook: stop at a point that pulls you back).
```

### 20. The Full Decomposition
```
Act as a task breakdown expert. My project: [PROJECT]. Decompose it into a dependency-ordered task list where NO task exceeds 25 minutes of focused work or one decision — the rule: if a task has an "and" in its description, split it. Mark each task with its dependency (what must finish first), the milestone checkpoints where I stop and review (say what I check at each), and estimate the total focused hours. Then: (1) the single first task I can do in the next hour — designed to be trivially completable, (2) the critical path — the tasks that determine the timeline, and (3) the parallel track — what can be done anytime (the waiting-room tasks). Output as a numbered list I could hand to a stranger.
```

### 21. Follow-Through System
```
You are a follow-through systems designer. I start strong and abandon projects: [PASTE EXAMPLES]. Diagnose my abandonment pattern: (1) where the energy drops in a typical project arc — week 2? the first obstacle? the 80% mark? — identify the recurring point, (2) what happens right before I quit — the trigger (a missed day, a bad result, a shiny new project). Then design the system: (1) smaller commitments — the version of the next project that finishes in half the time, (2) a visible progress tracker — the format I'll actually maintain ([FORMAT]), (3) an accountability structure — who/what, and the exact check-in, and (4) the pre-commitment device that makes quitting expensive: the specific stake ([MONEY, PUBLIC COMMITMENT, PARTNER — pick what works for me and say why]). Include the relapse protocol: what to do in the first 24 hours after missing a day.
```

### 22. Habit Design, 4-Week Ladder
```
Act as a habit formation coach using the evidence-based mechanics: cue, craving, response, reward. My desired habit: [HABIT]; current obstacle: [OBSTACLE]. Design it: (1) the habit stack — "After [EXISTING HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]" — anchored to a habit I already do reliably (list 3 candidate anchors from my routine), (2) the 2-minute version — the embarrassingly small start that makes refusing harder than doing, (3) the minimum viable version — what counts on a bad day, defined now so the streak survives, (4) the environment cue — the physical change (place the object, remove the friction: [SPECIFIC CHANGE]), and (5) the 4-week progression ladder — week 1: 2-minute version daily; week 2: [LEVEL]; week 3: [LEVEL]; week 4: full habit. Include the never-miss-twice rule.
```

### 23. Automation Triage
```

For each task: decide AUTOMATE, BATCH, TEMPLATE, or ELIMINATE — with the reasoning (frequency × time × error-proneness), and the specific tool and setup steps for the automate/batch calls. Rank all by hours saved per month — show the math per task. Then the implementation plan: the order (start with the one that saves the most per setup-hour), the time investment to set each up, and the maintenance cost. Include the trap: the task that looks automatable but changes too often (list any from my list with that profile) — template or batch it instead.
```

## Review & Reflection Systems (Prompts 29–34)

### 29. The Weekly Review, Scripted
```
You are a weekly review facilitator. Run my weekly review as a scripted agenda: (1) empty all inboxes — email, notes, Slack: to 30 minutes max, the quick-sort rules (delete/2-minute/do/delegate/schedule), (2) review last week's commitments — kept vs. slipped, and the honest cause of each slip (one line, no self-flagellation), (3) choose next week's 3 outcomes — not tasks: outcomes, stated as done-visible results, (4) check the calendar ahead — the two-week lookahead for conflicts, prep needs, and travel, (5) pick the one thing that would make next week a win. Deliver: the printable checklist, the 45-minute agenda with time allocations per step, and the "worst week" 15-minute version — what to do when Friday is already lost.
```

### 30. Monthly Reflection, 20 Minutes
```
Act as a monthly reflection partner. Guide my month review with these prompts — write the actual questions, not topics: (1) proudest accomplishment — what did I do and what does it say I should do more of? (2) biggest time sink — where did hours go that produced nothing? (3) what I said yes to that I shouldn't have — and the pattern behind it (who, what kind of ask), (4) energy audit — what drained me, what filled me: name the top of each list, (5) the one experiment for next month — a small, time-boxed test with a defined end. Keep the whole ritual to 20 minutes with a 5-line journal template. Then the honesty tool: the question I should answer but will want to skip — write it.
```

### 31. Quarterly Life Audit
```
You are a quarterly life audit designer. Build my audit across 5 areas: health, relationships, work, finances, growth. For each area: (1) the 1–10 rating — with the evidence that justifies the number (not vibes: what happened this quarter that supports it), (2) one evidence-based win — keep doing it, (3) one leak — the thing quietly costing me, named specifically, and (4) one next-quarter intention — a behavior, not a hope. Then the self-deception catchers: for each area, the question that cuts through the story I tell myself ("What would [someone who loves me] say this area really looks like?"). Format: one page per area, one summary page. Include the 90-minute session structure with breaks.
```

### 32. The 5-Number Dashboard
```
Act as a metrics-for-humans advisor. I want to track my life without becoming a spreadsheet person. Design the lightweight personal dashboard: (1) 5 numbers max — one per priority area ([LIST YOUR AREAS — e.g., sleep, deep work hours, workouts, money stress, quality time]), each defined precisely (what counts, what doesn't), (2) the capture method — each recordable in under 30 seconds daily: no app installs, paper or a single notes file, (3) the weekly rollup ritual — 5 minutes, one line: what the numbers say, (4) the monthly review question: "Is tracking this changing any decision?" — and the kill rule: metrics that don't change decisions get dropped (name which would likely die first), and (5) what I explicitly do NOT track — the list of things that get worse when measured.
```

### 33. Personal Retrospective
```
You are a lesson-learned facilitator. I just finished [PROJECT/SEASON]. Run my personal retrospective: (1) what WORKED — keep: name it specifically enough to repeat (not "planning" but "the Sunday 30-minute week plan"), (2) what HURT — kill: what cost me time, energy, or relationships, (3) what SURPRISED me — learn: the assumptions that were wrong (mine, specifically), (4) what I'll change next time — the difference between knowing and doing: the trigger that makes the change actually fire next time. Then turn the top 3 lessons into rules I write in my own words — imperative, short, slightly harsh: "Never start a [X] without [Y]" format. Close with the one-line motto for the next project — make it memorable, make it mine.
```

### 34. The Annual Review
```
Act as a year-in-review architect. Design my annual review in 90 minutes: (1) the 10 highlights reel — walk month by month first (calendar + photos + sent emails as memory prompts), then pick, (2) the year's implicit theme — the pattern that ran under it all, named in 3 words, (3) goals hit vs. abandoned — for the abandoned ones: the honest autopsy — dead from what? (lost interest = wrong goal; lost momentum = wrong system; lost time = wrong priority), (4) the relationships that deepened — and the ones that faded: what I did differently, (5) the one word for next year — with the selection criteria: what I need more of, not what sounds nice. ## Team & Delegation (Prompts 35–40)

### 35. The Delegation Audit
```
You are a delegation coach. I'm doing [TASKS] that others could do. For each task, decide: DELEGATE, AUTOMATE, or KEEP (only what truly needs me — apply the test: does this need MY judgment, or just A trustworthy person?). For each delegated task, write the handoff brief: (1) the outcome definition — what "done well" looks like in observable terms (not "handle the invoicing" but "invoices sent within 3 days of month end, zero errors, flagged exceptions to me"), (2) the constraints — budget, tools, brand rules, the lines they can't cross, (3) the checkpoints — weekly at first, then the graduation criteria for backing off, and (4) the decision rights — what they can decide without me, listed explicitly (the ambiguity here is where delegation dies). End with my first two weeks: what I do when they ask a question I could answer faster myself.
```

### 36. 1:1s That Matter
```
Act as a manager-of-one advisor for my direct reports. Help me run 1:1s that matter: (1) the 30-minute structure — THEIR agenda first (20 min), my items second (5 min), growth third (5 min) — the order is the point, (2) the 10 standing questions that surface problems early: write them out, including the 2 that feel awkward ("what's something I do that makes your job harder?"), (3) the note system — one running doc per person: what matters to them (kid's name, the project they want, what they're worried about) — so I remember without being reminded, and (4) what to do with recurring cancellations — the script that addresses it without shaming: the reschedule rule and the floor ("a 15-minute walk counts"). End with the anti-pattern: turning 1:1s into status meetings.
```

### 37. Team Operating Rhythm
```
You are a team cadence designer. My team of [SIZE] needs an operating rhythm: design each ritual — (1) daily standup: keep/kill/reformat — if reformatted: the async version (written, by [TIME], 3 lines: done/doing/blocked), (2) weekly priority setting: the 30-minute session, what's decided, (3) monthly demo: what gets shown, who watches, the celebration component, (4) quarterly planning: the half-day agenda. For each: duration, agenda, what's async vs. live, and the anti-pattern to avoid (standup = status theater; weekly = re-litigating priorities; demo = only wins shown). Include the escape valve: how a team member calls a needed meeting outside the rhythm. End with the time math: total ritual hours per person per week — and what to cut if it exceeds 4.
```

### 38. Difficult Feedback Script
```
Act as a feedback facilitator. I need to give [DIFFICULT FEEDBACK] to [PERSON] about [SITUATION]. Write the conversation script using SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact): (1) the situation — specific time and place, not "you always," (2) the behavior — observable facts, no interpretations ("you sent the deck at 11pm" not "you don't respect deadlines"), (3) the impact — on me, the team, the work — stated without accusation, (4) the question that invites their perspective: write it out — and my LISTENING plan for the answer (don't rebut, take notes, say "tell me more"), (5) the closing that sets expectations: what changes, by when, how we'll check. Include: the follow-up email template (2 hours after), and what to do if they get defensive — the de-escalation line and the pause-and-reschedule option.
```

### 39. Async Communication Norms
```
You are an async communication designer. My team wastes hours in meetings and pings. Build our async norms: (1) the channel rules — what belongs in docs (decisions, plans, anything referenced twice), chat (time-sensitive, quick answers), email (external, formal), meetings (real decisions needing real debate — the test: is this a conversation or a broadcast?), (2) the decision-record format — one page: context, options, decision, owner, date — the template written out, (3) response-time expectations per channel: chat = same day, docs comments = 2 days, email = 2 days — and the "this is not urgent" convention, (4) the no-meeting focus blocks: which days, protected how, and (5) the good async update template — written out: what I did, what's next, where I'm stuck, no response needed.
```

### 40. Hire vs. Delegate-First
