## POST 1 — "The prompt I run every Friday" (Day 2, Wed)

I've bought more productivity tools than I want to admit.

The one ritual that actually stuck costs nothing and takes 20 minutes on a Friday.

One AI prompt:

"Act as my weekly review partner. I'll paste my notes from the week.

1. Summarize what moved forward vs. what stalled.
2. Identify the one decision I'm avoiding.
3. Draft next week's top 3 priorities based on the above."

Line 2 is the whole trick.

Your to-do list only organizes what you've already decided to do. It's structurally blind to the things you haven't decided yet. Forcing a weekly search for the avoided decision surfaces them anyway.

It's uncomfortable every time. It's also right almost every time.

To be fair about what this is NOT: it's not a 10x system, and I won't pretend it transformed my calendar. It's one honest 20-minute meeting with myself where the machine takes the minutes.

The gains are boring and compounding: fewer avoided decisions, a shorter "stalled" list each week.

That's the whole post. If you try it for a month, I'd like to hear whether line 2 hits for you the way it hits for me.

## POST 2 — "Technique vs. template" (Day 4, Fri)

A year of prompting daily taught me one uncomfortable thing about "prompt engineering."

The techniques everyone debates — role assignment, context windows, chain-of-thought, output formats — are all correct.

They just don't survive contact with a Monday morning.

What survives: dumb fill-in-the-blank templates for recurring tasks.

A technique is "add role + context + constraints + examples."

A template is that same technique, pre-baked for one specific recurring task, with holes where your context goes:

"Write a 10-minute script about [TOPIC] with a hook, intro, 3 main points, and a call to action. Audience: [WHO]. Tone: [TONE]."

Same structure as the fancy version. Ten seconds to deploy. And the placeholders make it impossible to skip the context — which is exactly what most of us skip when we're busy, and exactly why our AI output comes back generic.

The rule I use now: if a prompt survives four weeks in my rotation, it graduates to a template file. Everything else was technique-flavored procrastination.

The boundary matters: one-off tasks ("respond to this angry client email") still need technique. Recurring tasks need templates. Treating one-offs like recurring work — or vice versa — is where the wasted hours live.

## POST 3 — "The $0 stack" (Day 6, Sun or Mon)

I shipped a digital product with $0 in software spend.

The full stack:

— Product: plain markdown files
— Landing page: hand-written HTML (one page)
— Hosting: GitHub Pages (free)
— Payments: Gumroad (10% + $0.50 per sale, no monthly fee)

That's it. No framework, no build pipeline, no SaaS subscriptions.

A few things a $0 stack teaches you that a funded stack doesn't:

1. Constraints force clarity. When hosting is free, you stop engineering infrastructure you don't need and start answering "what is this and who is it for" — which is the only question the first visitor asks.

2. Markdown was a product decision, not a laziness decision. Buyers of a prompt product want to copy-paste. A PDF would have cost a weekend and removed the one feature that matters.

3. Knowing the fee math changes your pricing confidence. On a $9.99 pack, Gumroad takes ~$1.50. On the $27.99 bundle, ~$3.30. No monthly burn while you find out what sells is the actual feature.

What $0 can't do (honest list): email capture (no backend), upsell flows, landing-page A/B tests. I'm not pretending those don't matter — they matter the day after you've validated demand. Not the day before.
